# Sovereign Human Authority — SHA

## Definition

**Sovereign Human Authority (SHA)** is the governing principle that final authority over consequential decisions remains with designated humans, even when AI systems perform most of the research, analysis, planning or execution work.

SHA is an **authority model**, not a claim about superior human cognition.

## Why It Exists

Advanced AI can create a dangerous ambiguity:

> The system that produces the most sophisticated recommendation can begin to look like the system that has the right to decide.

SFII rejects that equivalence.

Capability and authority are separate dimensions.

## SHA Rules

### 1. Authority must be explicit

No consequential authority is inferred from:

- model capability
- model confidence
- speed
- historical accuracy
- majority agent agreement
- previous approvals
- access to tools
- possession of credentials

### 2. Delegation is bounded

Humans may delegate:

- analysis
- monitoring
- drafting
- simulation
- reversible execution
- recurring low-risk operations

Delegation must specify:

- scope
- duration
- permissions
- decision class
- revocation path
- verification requirements

### 3. Authority does not self-expand

An AI system cannot:

- broaden its own scope
- lower its own approval threshold
- add new execution permissions
- redefine a decision as lower risk
- appoint itself or another AI as sovereign authority

### 4. Consequential decisions remain attributable

A consequential decision should have:

- identifiable human authority
- recorded proposal
- material evidence
- known dissent
- required approvals
- execution record
- outcome verification

### 5. Emergency authority must also be governed

Emergency mechanisms need:

- narrow activation criteria
- limited duration
- explicit logging
- post-event review
- revocation

“Emergency” must not become a permanent bypass.

## SHA Is Not

SHA is not:

- human micromanagement
- rejection of automation
- a requirement for unanimous human approval
- a belief that humans are unbiased
- a ban on autonomous low-risk operations
- a claim that cryptography creates legitimacy
- a substitute for legal governance

## Design Test

A proposed SFII component fails the SHA test if any of these are true:

- it can execute a consequential action without the required human authority
- it can silently alter its own authority
- its recommendation and execution roles are inseparable
- its evidence provenance cannot be inspected
- it can suppress dissent that should reach the decision maker
- revocation depends on the same component being revoked

## Practical Objective

The target is not “human in the loop” as a ceremonial click.

The target is:

**human authority that remains meaningful even when AI performs most of the work.**
